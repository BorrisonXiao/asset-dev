Proposal · RL dynamic downsampling

Cross-lingual 24k-step replication: Mandarin and Japanese

Replicate the LS960 24k-step learned-downsampling study in two languages whose natural acoustic-unit rates differ from English, with the minimal baseline set: fixed k=5, the char-level oracle (zh characters, ja moras), and the phone oracle. Revision 1 (2026-09-18): phone-anchored initialization, so the rate band can only compress — never inflate — the boundary rate. Revision 2 (2026-09-18): the initial/final arm is deferred for time, and the band is lowered to [0.10, 0.20] (5–10 Hz) so the learned arm operates closer to the char-level rate. Launch approved; jobs are being prepared and submitted.

1 · Question and rationale

Does learned boundary placement transfer outside English — and does it still pay off when the language's natural unit rate sits on the other side of the fixed-rate control?

The completed LS960 study showed that a learned Transformer-AR segmenter with BiGRU pooling (2.79±0.02 / 5.63±0.12 WER at ≈10.9 Hz) matches the character-oracle system and clearly beats fixed k=5. All of that evidence is in one language. English is stress-timed, and its character-CTC oracle runs at ≈14.6 Hz (kept ratio \(\rho\approx0.29\), where \(\rho\) is emitted boundaries per 50 Hz encoder frame, so the audio-token rate is \(50\rho\) Hz and lower \(\rho\) means more compression) — above the 10 Hz fixed k=5 grid. Both notations appear together throughout this page.

The two added languages invert or shift that relationship, which is exactly what makes the replication informative:

  • Mandarin is syllable-timed; one written character is one syllable, spoken at ≈5–6 /s, so the character oracle sits at roughly \(\rho\approx0.11\) (≈5.5 Hz) — below the fixed k=5 grid. If placement in the 5–10 Hz band still wins, the gain is not an artifact of English character rate exceeding the control rate.
  • Japanese is mora-timed at ≈7–8 mora/s (\(\rho\approx0.15\), ≈7.5 Hz), between the two. Its phone rate is high (mostly CV moras), separating the phone oracle from the mora oracle more than in English.

With English, the study then spans the three canonical rhythm classes (stress-, syllable-, and mora-timed), and each language contributes the same four-arm contrast: fixed grid vs. two alignment oracles vs. learned placement, all sharing one encoder and one decoder within the language.

4 · Boundary targets

Oracle targets reuse the LibriSpeech tooling (prepare_boundary_targets.py / ctc_boundary_align.py from the forced_alignment branch); the 50 Hz frame formula is identical for HuBERT-Large. Only the unit inventories are language-specific.

  • Phone oracle (also the initialization source). MFA with the best released acoustic models: the latest v3 mandarin_mfa and japanese_mfa models and their dictionaries, versions pinned at download and recorded in the alignment stats. AISHELL-1 transcripts are unsegmented character strings, so zh runs jieba word segmentation before MFA lookup; ja uses MFA's own tokenization. OOV words fall back to the models' own G2P; OOV counts are reported. Runs on the CPU partition. Because the learned arm initializes here, the S1 gate additionally checks \(\rho_{\text{phone}} \ge \rho_{hi} = 0.20\) (≥ 10 Hz) in each language.
  • zh character (syllable) oracle. Character-CTC forced alignment on frozen chinese-hubert-large features (AISHELL-1 train has a ≈4.2k character inventory — routine for Mandarin CTC). One character = one syllable.
  • ja mora oracle. Raw Japanese orthography mixes kanji and kana: a 3k+ character CTC over 100 h would align rare kanji unreliably, and one kanji spans several moras. Instead transcripts are converted to katakana readings (fugashi + unidic-lite) and aligned with a ≈150-unit mora-CTC. Mora is Japanese's natural sub-word rhythm unit, exactly parallel to zh character = syllable (Section 9, choice 3).
  • CTC-oracle reliability gates (revision 2). The char-level CTC aligners must prove themselves before any arm trains on their boundaries: (i) the aligner's own greedy dev CER ≤ 15% (an aligner that cannot roughly read the speech cannot place its units); (ii) ≥ 80% of char/mora boundaries fall within ±40 ms of an MFA phone boundary — characters and moras are composed of whole phones, so their onsets must coincide with phone onsets; (iii) per-unit duration distributions must be plausible (no mass at one frame, no several-second units).
  • Coverage and rates. Coverage ≥ 99.5% of utterances per split, and measured \(\rho\) for every unit reported against Table 4's predictions. A large deviation flags an alignment bug before any GPU training starts.
  • Deferred (revision 2): the zh initial/final (onset–rime) intermediate oracle — ≈1.85 units/syllable ≈ 10 Hz via pypinyin — is specified but not run in this round.

Revision 2 lowers the zh/ja band to [0.10, 0.20] (5–10 Hz): the floor sits at the zh syllable rate and the top at the fixed control, so both char-level rates are attainable inside the band and the learned arm operates closer to the char-level rate than the English band would allow. 5 · Curriculum and budget

The learned arm replicates the LS960 step24k curriculum stage for stage; only corpus-size-dependent knobs are re-derived, and each mapping is stated explicitly.

0

Cold start (per language, seed 3407, shared)

Supervised phone-oracle cold start of the Transformer-AR policy (BCE against MFA phone boundaries), 6 epochs at 400 s batches (≈8.1k steps on AISHELL — matching the ≈8.6k-step single-pass LS960 cold start), select best dev boundary F1 against the phone reference. All three RL seeds share it, as on LS960. LS960 cold-started on its finest reliable unit (characters, \(\rho=0.29\) ≈ 14.6 Hz); phones play that role here.

1

Decoder initialization + in-run warmup

Each learned seed initializes its decoder from the phone-oracle arm's dev-selected checkpoint of the same seed, then trains decoder-only CE on argmax boundaries for the first 2,400 optimizer steps — 10% of the budget, the same share as LS960's 2,395-step warmup. The init unit is chosen by the invariant of Table 4: from a phone-rate start (\(\rho\approx0.26\text{–}0.30\), 13–15 Hz, \(\ge \rho_{hi}\)) the band can only pull the rate down into [0.10, 0.20] = 5–10 Hz, the same direction as the English geometry (char 0.29 → realized ≈0.22, i.e. 14.6 → ≈10.9 Hz) but with a longer compression journey, monitored by the Section 7 guard. Because 2,400 steps exceeds one epoch on these corpora, the existing fraction-of-first-epoch knob cannot express it; a small --warmup_optimizer_steps extension (absolute steps) is added with a unit test beside test_segmenter_step_schedule.py.

2

Joint GRPO to 24,000 steps

LS960 step24k settings with the revision-2 band: NLL reward, K=4 rollouts, rate band \(\rho\in[0.10,0.20]\) (5–10 Hz) with \(\lambda_{\text{cap}}=1.0\), rate_channel auto, lr 2e-4 (decoder) / 5e-5 (policy), entropy 0, BF16, 300 s batches, validation at warmup end and every 4k steps, selection by minimum dev CER. The realized \(\rho\) trajectory is logged at every validation and monitored by the Section 7 guard: it must move non-increasingly from the phone-rate init toward the band.

3

CE baselines

fixed k=5, char-level oracle (zh characters / ja moras), and phone oracle: 10 epochs CE with dev-selection at 500 s batches × grad-accumulation 2 — the protocol of the matched 100 h controls. At these corpus sizes 10 CE epochs (≈10 passes) is comparable data exposure to the learned arm's 24k steps (≈13 passes at 300 s), and CE converges well within it. Section 9 (choice 6) records the strict-parity alternative.

Budget parity is better than the original. On LS960 the baselines were single-pass and the report carried an explicit unequal-budget caveat. Here every arm is dev-selected inside a generous budget, so no arm is read at an arbitrary stopping point.

10 · Risks

  • Upsampling instead of downsampling (resolved by design). With a syllable-rate initialization under the revision-1 band, the floor would have rewarded emitting ≈50% more boundaries than the init distribution — upsampling by construction. The phone-anchored initialization (Table 4 invariant) plus the \(\rho\)-trajectory guard (Section 7) makes rate pressure strictly downward, and the revision-2 band [0.10, 0.20] (5–10 Hz) widens the init margin (\(\rho_{\text{init}}\approx0.26\text{–}0.30\) vs. \(\rho_{hi}=0.20\)).
  • Longer compression journey (new in revision 2). The learned arm must travel from the phone-rate init (13–15 Hz) down to 5–10 Hz — roughly twice the relative compression the English run performed (14.6 → ≈10.9 Hz). The decoder warmup adapts at the init rate, so early joint steps pair a compressing policy with a decoder trained at a higher rate; the 4k-step validations and the \(\rho\)-trajectory guard monitor the transient, and the dev-selected checkpoint protects the reported result from a slow transient.
  • ReazonSpeech transcript noise. Subtitle-derived labels inflate absolute CER and blur the mora oracle. Mitigations: encoder is in-domain by construction, JSUT provides a clean secondary test, and alignment coverage stats are a hard gate before training.
  • Llama-3.2 weakness in zh/ja. Handled by design choice 1's smoke gate. Note the failure mode to watch is script drift (e.g., pinyin or romaji output), not just high CER.
  • Domain gap zh. chinese-hubert-large is pre-trained on web audio, AISHELL-1 is clean read speech — an easier direction than the reverse, but it makes zh the weaker in-domain claim of the two languages.
  • More passes under RL. 24k steps is ≈13 passes here vs. ≈2 on LS960; the policy re-samples the same utterances more often. Dev-CER selection every 4k steps plus the existing joint-drift monitoring (selected-epoch bookkeeping) covers it; if drift appears, densify validation to 2k steps — no new machinery.
  • MFA dictionary coverage. OOV entries (AISHELL topic domains, ja proper nouns) get G2P fallback via the MFA models' own G2P; OOV counts are reported with the alignment stats.
